## Overview

The Paranoia Engine is an experimental game engine built around deterministic
simulation, AI-native gameplay development, and a world whose behavior can be
inspected, reproduced, and changed while it runs.

Written in Rust, its simulation operates independently of Bevy, which handles
rendering, input, and presentation. The same authoritative world can be driven
by a playable game, a headless test runner, or an automated development tool.

The engine explores two distinct uses of AI: autonomous agents that create and
modify gameplay mechanics, and local models that make tactical decisions for
characters inside the game.

Neither system is permitted to directly dictate authoritative world state.
Their outputs are evaluated through the engine's own rules, constraints, and
verification mechanisms.

Lockdown is the playable demonstration built to exercise these capabilities.

## Technical approach

- **Deterministic simulation.** Authoritative world state advances at a fixed
  60 Hz using integer-based positions, angles, energies, and other simulation
  quantities. Recorded inputs reproduce complete world states tick by tick.
- **Simulation independent of rendering.** Bevy displays the world and collects
  input, but the Rust simulation remains authoritative. The same system runs
  without graphics for testing, verification, and replay.
- **Gameplay as declarative content.** Weapons and other mechanics are defined
  through bounded rule packs rather than arbitrary executable code. The engine
  interprets their effects through shared material, damage, electrical, and
  device systems.
- **AI as an input, not an authority.** Development agents propose revisions;
  local NPC models select from engine-generated legal actions. The engine
  determines what can actually happen.
- **Evidence-driven development.** Proposed changes are evaluated against
  protected requirements, with explicit results for verified, tested,
  unresolved, and violated obligations.
- **Live, state-preserving changes.** Accepted revisions can be installed at
  a simulation tick boundary after checking the current running world,
  preserving entity identities, damage, position, and mission progress.

## Autonomous gameplay authoring

The engine exposes a structured development interface through typed
operations and the Model Context Protocol.

An AI development agent can inspect the world, discover supported mechanics,
propose new rules, evaluate their consequences, investigate failures, and
request integration.

The agent cannot directly promote its own proposals or rewrite the
developer-owned requirements through that interface.

One demonstration asks an agent to create an electromagnetic weapon. The
resulting effects aren't simply scripted visual events: electrical
propagation, device faults, mechanical consequences, and objective
accessibility emerge from the shared simulation.

A proposal can satisfy its immediate task while violating another
requirement. The engine reports those violations, including their observed
effects and recorded causal relationships, before allowing a revision to be
accepted.

An authorized revision is then evaluated against the live world's current
state. A change that was acceptable in an undamaged starting scenario may
be rejected after the player has altered the environment.

The mechanism remains experimental. Requirements are evaluated within bounded
scenarios, and the agent's tool restrictions are not equivalent to
operating-system isolation.

## Local AI for autonomous characters

The engine also integrates a local small language model for NPC
decision-making.

Rather than asking the model to generate unrestricted actions, the simulation
constructs a set of legal tactical choices. The model ranks those choices,
and the engine validates the selected action before applying it.

If a decision is stale, invalid, or no longer legal, the engine falls back
to a deterministic baseline policy.

The model's decision is recorded as an ordinary simulation input. That means
an entire encounter can be replayed without loading or invoking the model
again.

The current model uses Laya, a ModernBERT-based choice ranker fine-tuned using
encounters generated by the simulation itself.

In a held-out evaluation across 16 unseen geometries, the selected model
reduced normalized regret from 0.313 for the engine's baseline policy to
0.223, with grouped confidence intervals supporting an improvement on that
metric.

The results are not uniformly positive: another evaluated model performed
better on that final split, and the selected model did not demonstrate an
improvement in raw regret.

These evaluations measure tactical performance under the project's test
conditions, not general NPC intelligence.

## Reproducible worlds and counterfactuals

Every simulation session can be recorded as a sequence of commands and
canonical world states.

Replay supplies the commands to the simulation again and compares the
resulting state after each tick. A mismatch identifies the first divergent
tick and the fields that differ.

Recorded scenarios have also reproduced identical results across macOS on
Apple Silicon and Linux on x86-64.

This mechanism supports more than debugging.

The engine can return to a recorded NPC decision, substitute another legal
choice, and simulate the resulting alternative future.

In one recorded comparison, changing a guard's decision at tick 2504 changed
12 final-state fields, increased the number of shots fired from 32 to 45,
and prevented the guard from reaching a defensive position.

Later recorded decisions are reused only when their original observation
remains applicable. Otherwise, the engine treats them as invalidated by
the changed history.

The result is a counterfactual experiment whose differences can be traced
to a specific decision and reproduced independently.

## Lockdown: the playable demonstration

Lockdown brings the engine's systems together in a first-person facility
encounter.

The scenario includes weapons, structural damage, electrical systems,
power distribution, devices, environmental obstacles, and an objective.

During a recorded Phase 09 demonstration, a real AI development agent authored
an electromagnetic weapon through the engine's tool interface. Its proposal
satisfied the six protected showcase requirements, and the game installed
the resulting revision while the session continued.

The resulting electromagnetic discharge triggered a chain of environmental
consequences, while locally controlled guards continued responding to events.

The recorded session includes 4,270 simulation ticks and was verified by
headless replay on Linux without the AI models present.

For that automated demonstration, integration was preauthorized by the test
harness rather than approved by a human operator during the session.

The presentation itself remains a prototype. Its automated route has been
exercised, but a complete human-paced rehearsal has not yet been recorded.

## Material Reality — experimental research

A newer development effort investigates physical destruction through material
composition rather than conventional hit points.

Material Reality represents assemblies as combinations of materials,
construction details, and physical state. Interactions can produce persistent
perforations, mechanical failures, thermal changes, and other consequences
that affect later interactions.

A projectile passing through an existing hole encounters a different physical
situation from one striking intact material. The subsystem also tracks
material mass through the resulting changes.

The experiments use deterministic histories and controlled counterfactuals
to compare what happens when individual material or construction properties
change.

This work is not yet a general-purpose physical simulation system. Its material
parameters remain uncalibrated, its representation has geometric limitations,
and the more complete rendered demonstration exists on an unmerged experimental
development line.

Material Reality is an exploration of how a game world might respond
consistently to its physical composition, rather than relying on unrelated,
hand-authored damage behaviors.

## Status and limitations

The Paranoia Engine is a research prototype, not a production-ready
general-purpose game engine.

The audited native test run passed 307 tests. The completed Phase 09
verification records include cross-platform replay, agent-authoring
demonstrations, NPC evaluation, and recorded presentation runs.

Important limitations remain:

- The engine's fixed-point simulation is deterministic for the demonstrated
  cases, but not every authoritative arithmetic operation has explicit
  overflow checking.
- Agent-authored mechanics use a deliberately restricted vocabulary of
  triggers, conditions, and effects.
- Protected requirements are checked through structural properties and
  bounded scenarios, not general formal proofs.
- Agent isolation is enforced through its available development tools, not
  an operating-system sandbox.
- The local NPC model ranks engine-generated decisions; its capabilities
  do not extend to unrestricted planning or action generation.
- The presentation has known frame-time spikes and has not completed a
  human-paced rehearsal.
- Material Reality, Many Worlds, and the expanded causal analysis remain
  experimental work outside the merged Phase 09 baseline.
- Several assets, model weights, and supporting demonstrations require
  external bundles that are not included in the source repository.

The project is being developed in progressively verified phases, with
determinism, explicit simulation semantics, and reproducible evidence
treated as foundational constraints.

## Resources

- The Paranoia Engine source repository is currently private.
- The implementation uses Rust for its authoritative simulation, Bevy for
  rendering, and a Python-hosted local model worker. The engine will be
